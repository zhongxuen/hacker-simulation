## The one-sentence version

<Term id="scope">Scope</Term> is the agreed list of which systems you may test, how, and when.
Everything outside it is off limits.

## Why it matters in security

<Term id="authorization">Authorization</Term> says you may test. Scope says exactly what. Step one
inch outside it and you're testing without permission again, with all the legal trouble that brings.

Scope also keeps a test safe. A client might leave out a fragile machine that can't handle a scan, or a system run by another company. Testing it anyway could break something the client depends on, or reach into someone else's property.

## How it actually works

Scope is written down before the test starts, as part of the <Term id="rules-of-engagement">rules of engagement</Term>. It usually names:

- **The targets**: exact addresses, network ranges, website addresses or accounts.
- **The exclusions**: things that are nearby but off limits, named so nobody wonders.
- **The methods**: for example, scanning is allowed but trying passwords isn't.
- **The dates and times**: permission starts and ends.

When a tester finds something outside the scope, like an unexpected computer that belongs to another company, they stop. They write down what they saw, they don't touch it, and they ask the client. If the client wants it tested and can agree to it, the scope letter gets updated first.

## Common misconceptions

- **"If I can reach it from inside the scope, it's in scope."** Reaching something and being allowed to test it are different things. The letter decides, not the network.
- **"It's in the same building, so the owner can agree to it."** Owners can only agree to what they own or control. Customers' phones, a neighbour's wifi or another company's server need their own permission.
- **"A quick look outside the scope doesn't count."** Scanning or logging in to something outside the scope is testing it. If it's not on the letter, ask first.
- **"Scope is paperwork, not security."** Scope is what keeps a test from breaking something the client needs, and it's what makes the test legal.
