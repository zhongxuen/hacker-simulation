## The one-sentence version

A careful system never keeps your password. It keeps a <Term id="hash">hash</Term> of it, mixed with a pinch of randomness called a <Term id="salt">salt</Term>, so that even the people who run the system can't read what you typed.

Think of a cloakroom that photographs your coat instead of hanging on to it. The photo is enough to match you to your coat when you come back. To a thief who steals the photo album, it's an album of coats nobody can wear.

That's the idea. Here's the part people find genuinely strange:

## Why it matters in security

Every few weeks, a company announces a break-in. What happens to you next depends almost entirely on one decision they made years earlier.

If they stored <Term id="plaintext">plaintext</Term> passwords, the thief now has a working password for every account — and for every _other_ site where that person used the same one. If they stored salted hashes made with a slow recipe, the thief has a file of fingerprints that would take lifetimes to guess through, and everybody has time to change their password calmly.

That's why "we were broken into, and passwords were safe" is a real sentence rather than an excuse. It's not a claim that nothing was stolen. It's a claim that what was stolen is close to useless, and it has to be earned in advance.

For your team, this is one of the most valuable findings you can hand a client: how they store passwords today, and what it would cost them to store them better. It's <Term id="confidentiality">confidentiality</Term> that holds up even after the door has been forced.

## How it actually works

**Signing up.** You choose a password. The system makes up a fresh random salt for you, mixes it with your password, hashes the result, and stores the salt and the hash. Your password itself is never written down anywhere.

**Signing in.** You type your password. The system fetches your salt, mixes it in the same way, hashes it, and compares the two hashes. Match, and you're in. It has checked your password without ever knowing it.

### Why the salt matters so much

Picture a thousand people at a company, and say eleven of them picked `sunshine`.

Without salt, those eleven rows are _identical_. An attacker who works out one has worked out eleven, and they can spot the popular passwords by looking for repeated rows before guessing anything at all. Worse, they can guess for a whole industry at once: a hash of `sunshine` is the same everywhere in the world, so a list of pre-hashed common passwords cracks every unsalted site ever stolen.

With salt, each of those eleven rows looks unrelated to the others. The pre-made lists are worthless, and every account has to be guessed on its own. One pinch of randomness turns one guess-for-all into a thousand separate jobs.

### Why the recipe matters too

Salt stops the shortcut. It doesn't slow down the guessing itself, and that's a separate lever.

The fast recipes — MD5, SHA-256 — were built to be fast, because checking a file's fingerprint should be quick. That speed is a gift to an attacker with a stolen file, who can try enormous numbers of guesses. The password recipes — bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2 — are deliberately slow and can be told to run slower as computers get faster. A delay nobody notices at sign-in is a wall when it's multiplied by billions of guesses.

Strong storage is both together: **a slow recipe, salted, one salt per account.**

## In practice

In [Reading the machine](/missions/linux-01) you meet the opposite of all this: a password sitting in a configuration file in readable text, because a service needed it and nobody thought further. That file has no hash, no salt, and no recipe — and everyone on the server can open it.

The pattern to carry into any mission: find where the secrets live, say what form they're in, and say what better would look like. You'll have a real answer for the second and third parts now.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The salt has to be kept secret."** It doesn't, and it usually sits right next to the hash. A salt isn't a second password; its job is to make two identical passwords store differently. Keeping it secret would be a bonus, not the point.
- **"Salting makes each password harder to guess."** Not on its own. Your password is exactly as guessable as it was — salt removes the attacker's _shortcuts_ across accounts. The slow recipe is what makes each guess expensive. You want both.
- **"We hash with SHA-256, so we're fine."** SHA-256 is a good, sound recipe doing the job it was built for: fingerprinting files, fast. That speed is what makes it the wrong tool here. Use one built for passwords instead.
- **"If they email me a reset link, they can see my password."** A reset link proves the opposite. They can't read your password, so they're offering you a way to set a new one.
- **"Hashed passwords are uncrackable, so a stolen file doesn't matter."** Salted and slow is excellent protection, and it isn't a force field. Someone whose password is `bakery2024` is still in trouble, which is why a break-in announcement still asks everyone to change theirs. See [Passwords, and why a second step beats a longer one](/learn/auth-passwords-and-mfa).
- **"This only matters for big companies."** Every system with a sign-in page stores passwords somehow, including a community theatre's box office computer. Small organisations are where you'll find the readable files, because nobody there was ever told this. <Term id="dictionary-attack">Dictionary attacks</Term> don't check how big you are, and neither does <Term id="password-cracking">password cracking</Term>.
