## The one-sentence version

Your <Term id="browser">browser</Term> has a rule that a web page may only read things from its own website, not from other websites you happen to have open. It's called the <Term id="same-origin-policy">same-origin policy</Term>, and it's one of the most important protections on the web.

Think of tabs as flats in one building. Each flat reads its own post. A note someone slips under your door can't open your neighbour's letters.

## Why it matters in security

Your browser is signed in to lots of things at once: email, shopping, work tools. Each of those sites trusts your browser because it carries your <Term id="cookie">cookies</Term>. Without a rule keeping sites apart, any page you opened could quietly ask your email for your messages, with your cookies attached, and read the answer.

Many web attacks are attempts to get around this rule, and many web defences are about keeping it strong.

## How it actually works

An origin is three parts of a <Term id="url">URL</Term> (a web address) taken together. Pick each part:

Two pages have the same origin only if scheme, host and port all match. When a page asks for something from a different origin, the browser still lets it send some requests, like loading a picture or submitting a form, but it won't let the page read the answer.

A website that wants to share with other origins can say so, with a response header called CORS (Cross-Origin Resource Sharing). The browser relaxes the rule only as far as that header allows.

Where the flats analogy breaks down: the rule is enforced by the browser, not the website. A program that isn't a browser, like `webprobe`, can ask any site for anything it's allowed to reach. The policy protects people browsing, not the website itself.

## In practice

When a tester finds a way to make a site show visitors text that their browser runs as code, called <Term id="cross-site-scripting">cross-site scripting</Term>, the report explains it in these terms: the attacker's code runs with the site's own origin, so the same-origin policy doesn't help. When you read `webprobe` output in a mission, response headers like `Set-Cookie` and any CORS headers are part of how a site draws its borders.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The same-origin policy stops all attacks between websites."** It stops a page reading another site's answers. It doesn't stop a page making some requests, which is why cookies also get the `SameSite` rule, and why forms that change things need extra checks.
- **"Same domain name, same origin."** `http://` and `https://`, or two different ports, are different origins even on the same name.
- **"It's a setting on the website."** It's built into every browser. Websites can only loosen it for themselves, with CORS headers, and getting those headers too loose is a common finding.
- **"If a site is on HTTPS, the policy doesn't matter."** HTTPS and the same-origin policy do different jobs: one guards the network, the other guards the tabs from each other.
