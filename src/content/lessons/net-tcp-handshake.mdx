## The one-sentence version

Before two computers exchange anything important, they greet each other in three quick steps to make sure both are listening. That greeting is the <Term id="tcp-handshake">TCP handshake</Term>.

It's like starting a phone call on a bad line. "Hello, can you hear me?" "Yes, I hear you. Can you hear me?" "Yes!" Now you talk. Pick each step to see what it does:

## Why it matters in security

The handshake is how a port scan learns that a door is open. A scanner sends step 1 to a port. If a service is listening, step 2 comes back. If nothing is listening, the computer says "no one here", and if a firewall is in the way, often nothing comes back at all. Those three answers, open, closed and silent, are what every scan result is built on.

Defenders use the same fact the other way. Hundreds of step-1 messages from one address to many doors, with no real conversation after, is what a scan looks like in the logs. It's often the first sign that someone is looking around.

## How it actually works

Messages on a network travel in small chunks called <Term id="packet">packets</Term>, each with a label saying where it's from, where it's going, and which port it's for. The rules for how those packets are used form a <Term id="protocol">protocol</Term>: an agreed way of talking, like the etiquette of a phone call.

<Term id="tcp">TCP</Term> (Transmission Control Protocol) is the careful protocol. It starts every
conversation with the handshake, numbers every packet, and asks again for anything that goes
missing. Websites, email and remote logins all use it, because a web page with a missing chunk is no
good to anyone.

Its quicker cousin, <Term id="udp">UDP</Term> (User Datagram Protocol), skips the handshake and the checking. It sends and hopes. That's fine for a video call, where a missing split second doesn't matter and waiting would be worse.

Where the phone-call analogy breaks down: a phone call is one conversation, but a computer can hold thousands of handshakes at once, one for each door and visitor. And nobody hears the greeting: it's over in a few thousandths of a second.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A port scan is an attack."** A scan asks which doors answer. It doesn't open them. That said, on someone else's network without permission it looks exactly like the first step of an attack, and it's treated that way.
- **"If there's no reply, there's nothing there."** Silence often means a firewall is dropping your knocks. The computer behind it may be busy serving everyone else.
- **"TCP is safer than UDP."** TCP is more reliable, not more secret. Neither scrambles anything on its own. Scrambling comes from protocols on top, like HTTPS, the secure way websites send pages (the S stands for secure).
- **"The handshake checks who you are."** It only checks that both sides are listening. Proving who you are, with a password or a key, comes later, inside the conversation.
