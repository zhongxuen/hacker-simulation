## The one-sentence version

Every computer on a network has an address, like a house on a street, so messages know where to go. That address is called its <Term id="ip-address">IP address</Term> (IP stands for Internet Protocol, the rules computers use to pass messages along).

Your practice computer has one too. Ask it:

<MiniTerminal
  scenario="range-network"
  commands={["ifconfig"]}
  task="Type `ifconfig` and press Enter, then look for the address after the word `inet`."
  expect="ifconfig"
  success="Your practice computer's address is `192.168.60.10`. Every message meant for it carries that number."

## Why it matters in security

You can't protect a computer you don't know is there. The first thing a defender does on a network is find out which addresses are in use, and what's behind each one. Attackers do the same thing, which is why a forgotten computer with an address nobody remembers is such a good hiding place for trouble.

Addresses also show up everywhere a security analyst looks: in logs ("someone signed in from `192.168.77.23`"), in firewall rules, and in scan results. Reading them quickly is a core skill.

## How it actually works

An IP address is four numbers from 0 to 255, separated by dots: `192.168.60.20`. Any computer with an address is called a <Term id="host">host</Term>.

Computers that sit on the same network usually share the first part of their address, like houses on the same street share a street name. That group of neighbouring addresses is a <Term id="subnet">subnet</Term>.

A range is written with a slash: `192.168.60.0/24`. The `/24` means "the first three numbers stay the same". So `192.168.60.0/24` is every address from `192.168.60.0` to `192.168.60.255`: 256 addresses, one street.

Where the house-number analogy breaks down: a computer can have more than one address (one for each network it's plugged into), and addresses can change when a computer reconnects. That's why analysts always note the time next to an address.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Private addresses like `192.168.x.x` are secret."** They're not secret, they're local. Thousands of homes and offices use the same `192.168.1.x` addresses inside their own walls. Knowing one tells an outsider almost nothing, and hiding it protects nothing.
- **"An address belongs to a person."** An address belongs to a connection, and it can move. A laptop gets a different address at home, at the bakery, and on the customer wifi. That's why investigators pair an address with a time.
- **"If a scan doesn't find a computer, it isn't there."** Some computers ignore scans, or sit behind rules that block you. A scan shows what answered you, not everything that exists.
