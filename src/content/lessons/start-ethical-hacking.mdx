## The one-sentence version

<Term id="ethical-hacking">Ethical hacking</Term> means testing how well a computer system is
protected, with the owner's permission, so its weak spots get fixed before a criminal finds them.

Picture a locksmith the homeowner hired to test the locks. A burglar and that locksmith might try the very same door. One was invited. The other wasn't.

## Why it matters in security

A <Term id="hacker">hacker</Term> is someone who understands computers well enough to make them do unexpected things. Some use that to steal, spy or cause harm. People call them <Term id="black-hat">black hats</Term>. Others use it to protect people, and only test what they've been asked to test. They're called <Term id="white-hat">white hats</Term>.

Criminals only need to find one weak spot. Defenders have to find all of them. So organisations invite white-hat teams to look first, then fix what they find. That's the job your team, Candlewright Security, does.

## How it actually works

Every real job starts with <Term id="authorization">authorization</Term>: a signed letter from the owner saying who may test what, how, and until when. Anything not on that letter is off limits, however interesting it looks.

When the test is done, the team writes a plain report: what they found, how serious it is, and how to fix it. Nothing found is kept, used or shared.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Hacking is always illegal."** Testing with the owner's written permission is a normal, well-paid job. Testing without it is the crime.
- **"If I only look and don't change anything, it's fine."** Looking around a computer without permission is already breaking in, in most countries.
- **"A computer anyone can reach is fair game."** Being able to reach something isn't the same as being allowed to test it. The letter decides, not the network.
