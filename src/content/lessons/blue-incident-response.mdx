## The one-sentence version

<Term id="incident-response">Incident response</Term> is the agreed set of steps a team follows when
something has gone wrong, decided in advance so that nobody is inventing it at 3 in the morning.

A <Term id="security-incident">security incident</Term> is anything that harms, or might harm, the safety of a system or the information in it. Most organisations in these missions have four or five staff and no security team at all, so their plan fits on one card:

## Why it matters in security

The damage from an incident is decided in the first hour, and almost all of it by people who aren't security specialists: whoever noticed, and whoever they told.

Two organisations get the same break-in. One has a card by the till and loses a morning. The other has four people trying to be helpful in different directions: one reinstalls the machine, one changes every password, one says nothing because they think they caused it. A week later nobody can say what happened, so nobody can say whether it's over.

## How it actually works

Four steps, in this order. The order is the part people get wrong.

### 1. Work out what's actually happening

Confirm it's real before the fire drill starts. Read the logs, look at what's running, check whether the strange connection is a backup job that's run every night for two years. Write down the time you started and what you saw. This step ends with one sentence: _something is wrong, here, and it looks like this._

### 2. Stop it spreading

<Term id="containment">Containment</Term> buys time. Unplug the machine, switch off the account,
block the address. It isn't a fix and doesn't pretend to be, and the questions worth settling in
advance are: who is allowed to unplug what, and who do they ring at 3 in the morning.

### 3. Get rid of it, then get working again

<Term id="eradication">Eradication</Term> means removing the cause and the way in: the harmful
program, the unpatched weak spot, the password that leaked, the old account nobody had removed. Then
recovery, which for a small business usually means restoring from a backup and watching that machine
more closely than usual for a fortnight. Skipping straight to recovery is how the same organisation
gets hit twice in a month.

### 4. Sit down afterwards and change one thing

The <Term id="post-incident-review">post-incident review</Term> is a calm meeting once it's over. What happened, in order. What made it possible. What changes, who does it, by when. The useful version asks what made the mistake possible rather than who made it: people who expect blame stop mentioning things early, and mentioning things early is worth more than anything said in that meeting.

## See it

Step one is reading, and reading is the part you can practise. This practice machine's sign-in log has a night on it.

Notice what you haven't done: nothing has been switched off, deleted or reinstalled. You read, and now you can describe it. That's the whole of step one.

## Common misconceptions

- **"We're too small for this."** Small organisations get broken into constantly, usually by something automated that had no idea who they were. The plan for a five-person bakery is one card, and writing it takes an afternoon once.
- **"The first step is getting rid of the malware."** The first step is understanding. Remove the harmful program without finding the way in, and you've bought a few days.
- **"Containment means the incident is over."** It means it stopped growing. The way in is still there until somebody closes it.
- **"The review is where we find out whose fault it was."** A review that hunts for a person teaches everyone to stay quiet next time. The good ones change a setting, a habit or a default, and name who's doing it.
- **"We'll know when something happens."** Most organisations find out from a customer, a bank or a stranger. That's an argument for having the card by the till, not for assuming the phone will stay quiet.
