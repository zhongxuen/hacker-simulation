## The one-sentence version

<Term id="threat-modelling">Threat modelling</Term> means sitting down before anything goes wrong
and asking what you're protecting, what could go wrong, and what you'll do about it.

It's like planning for a holiday. What's worth protecting at home? Who might come for it? How would they get in? And what will you do about each door and window?

## Why it matters in security

No team has time to defend everything equally. Threat modelling points the effort at what matters most: the things that are both likely to go wrong and harmful when they do. That combination is called <Term id="risk">risk</Term>.

It's also far cheaper to fix a hole in a plan than in a finished building. Teams that ask "what could go wrong?" early spend less time cleaning up later.

## How it actually works

A widely used version is four plain questions:

1. **What are we working on?** Draw it: the computers, the people, the data, and how they connect. An <Term id="asset-inventory">asset inventory</Term>, a list of every device and program you own, is the starting point.
2. **What can go wrong?** For each part, think of a <Term id="threat">threat</Term>: anyone or anything that could harm it. The CIA triad helps here: could someone read it, change it, or stop it working? Every place someone could try to get in adds to the <Term id="attack-surface">attack surface</Term>.
3. **What are we going to do about it?** For each threat: fix it, reduce it, watch for it, or knowingly accept it.
4. **Did we do a good job?** Check the answers later, and do it again when things change.

Where the holiday analogy breaks down: a house changes slowly. Computer systems change every week, so a threat model is never finished. It's updated.

## In practice

Candlewright sketches a threat model at the start of each engagement, from the client's point of view, so the testing aims at what the client cares about. In the mission [Mapping the network](/missions/net-01), you'll build the "what are we working on?" part by scanning the bakery's network, and the finding you report comes straight from "what can go wrong?".

Some teams use checklists to prompt question 2, like STRIDE, whose letters stand for six kinds of trouble: pretending to be someone else, tampering, denying you did something, leaking information, stopping a service, and gaining powers you shouldn't have.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Threat modelling is only for big companies."** A bakery's version fits on a whiteboard. The four questions work at any size.
- **"It means predicting exactly who will attack."** It's about what you have and where it's weak. Attackers change; your weak spots are what you can work on.
- **"You do it once."** Every new computer, website or supplier changes the answers. The fourth question exists to bring you back round.
- **"The goal is zero risk."** There's always some risk left. The goal is to know what it is, and to have chosen it on purpose.
