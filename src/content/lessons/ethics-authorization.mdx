## The one-sentence version

<Term id="authorization">Authorization</Term> is clear permission, usually in writing, from the
owner of a computer system to test it.

Picture a locksmith opening a front door. With a note from the owner, that's a job. Without one, it's a break-in, even though the locksmith's hands do exactly the same thing. Security testing works the same way. Try this situation:

## Why it matters in security

An <Term id="ethical-hacking">ethical hacker</Term> and a criminal can type the same commands. Permission is the whole difference between them.

Without it, testing someone else's computers is a crime in most countries. In the UK, the Computer Misuse Act makes accessing a computer without permission an offence. In the US, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act does something similar. Other countries have their own laws, and they differ in the details. The outcome can include being prosecuted, even if you meant well.

Permission also protects the owner. A test can slow a website down or set off alarms. When the owner has agreed in writing, they know it's coming, and they know who to call.

## How it actually works

Before a professional test, often called a <Term id="penetration-test">penetration test</Term>, the testing team and the owner agree on paper:

- **Who** is testing, and who at the organisation signed.
- **What** may be tested: which computers, websites or accounts.
- **How**: which kinds of tests are allowed, and which aren't.
- **When**: the dates and hours.
- **What happens if something goes wrong**, and who to phone.

Those ground rules are called the <Term id="rules-of-engagement">rules of engagement</Term>. The signature has to come from someone who has the authority to agree for the organisation. A receptionist, a friend who works there, or a stranger online can't give it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"If a website is on the internet, anyone may test it."** Being able to reach something isn't the same as being allowed to test it. A shop's door being on the street doesn't mean you may try its locks.
- **"I only looked, I didn't change anything."** In many countries, getting into a computer without permission is an offence on its own, even if you only read.
- **"My intentions were good, so it's legal."** Good intentions don't replace permission. Plenty of people who meant to help have still faced charges.
- **"A verbal yes is enough."** Professionals get it in writing, from someone with the authority to agree, because memories and phone calls don't hold up when something goes wrong.
