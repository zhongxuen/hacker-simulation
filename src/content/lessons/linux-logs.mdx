## The one-sentence version

A <Term id="log-file">log file</Term> is a computer's diary: every time something happens, a program writes one line about it, starting with the date and time.

It never tears out a page by itself, which is what makes it so useful after something goes wrong. Here are a few lines from the sign-in log on a practice computer:

## Why it matters in security

Logs are how defenders find out what happened, often days later. A sign-in at 2 in the morning from an address nobody recognises, or a burst of wrong passwords seconds apart, are the lines that start an investigation.

A burst of wrong passwords usually means someone is guessing, trying one password after another. That's called a <Term id="brute-force-attack">brute-force attack</Term>. Because logs give intruders away, they often try to delete them, which is why defenders send copies somewhere the intruder can't reach.

## How it actually works

On Linux, logs live in `/var/log`. Two you'll meet often:

- `auth.log`: every sign-in, good or bad, and every use of `sudo`.
- `syslog`: general news from the computer's programs.

Every line starts with a <Term id="timestamp">timestamp</Term>. In `2026-03-02T08:30:10Z`, the `T` separates the date from the time, and the `Z` means the time is in UTC, one shared world clock, so logs from computers in different places line up.

Logs can hold private details, so many are locked. `auth.log` belongs to root and the `adm` group, and only accounts in that group can read it.

You rarely read a whole log. You filter it:

- `grep Failed /var/log/auth.log` keeps only the lines containing "Failed".
- `tail -n 5 /var/log/auth.log` shows the last five lines, usually the newest.
- `logview /var/log/auth.log --grep failed` numbers the lines and filters them in one go.
- `sort /var/log/auth.log` puts the lines in time order, because a timestamp at the front sorts correctly.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Deleting the log hides what happened."** A missing log, or a gap in one, is a clue on its own. And good defenders keep a copy on another computer, out of an intruder's reach.
- **"Logs record everything."** They only record what programs are set up to write. If nobody turned on logging for something, there's no line about it.
- **"The newest line is always at the bottom."** Usually, but logs get merged and rotated. Trust the timestamps, not the position.
- **"Anyone can read the logs."** Many are locked to root and the `adm` group, because they hold names, addresses and other private details.
