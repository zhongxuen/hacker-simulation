## The one-sentence version

In most countries, getting into or testing a computer system without the owner's permission is a crime, whatever your reason.

## Why it matters in security

The difference between a <Term id="white-hat">white hat</Term>, who protects people, and a <Term id="black-hat">black hat</Term>, who breaks in, isn't skill or tools. It's <Term id="authorization">authorization</Term>: the owner's permission.

Laws in many countries make that line firm. The UK's Computer Misuse Act makes it an offence to access a computer without authorization. The US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act covers accessing computers without authorization, or going beyond what you're allowed. Many other countries have similar laws. The details differ from place to place, and this lesson isn't legal advice, but the direction is the same almost everywhere.

The consequences can be serious. They include being prosecuted, fines, prison in some cases, and a record that makes it hard to work in security later. That's why every professional test starts with a signature.

## How it actually works

These laws usually care about a few questions:

- **Did you have permission** from someone entitled to give it?
- **Did you go beyond it**, for example by testing a system that wasn't on the letter?
- **What did you do once in?** Reading, copying, changing or deleting data can each make things worse.

Notice what's missing: "Did you mean well?" and "Was the door left open?" A weak spot, called a <Term id="vulnerability">vulnerability</Term>, being there doesn't give anyone permission to use it. An unlocked door still isn't an invitation.

Real harm happens too. A test nobody expected can crash a website, lock people out of accounts, or send a security team into a panic at 2 in the morning. The people affected didn't agree to any of it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"If it's publicly reachable, it's fair game."** A website being on the internet invites you to use it the way it's meant to be used. It doesn't invite you to test its defences.
- **"Scanning isn't hacking."** Scanning someone else's network can look like the first step of an attack, and in some countries it can be an offence on its own. Professionals only scan networks they have permission for.
- **"I only looked, I didn't change anything."** Getting in without permission can be a crime even if you don't touch a thing.
- **"Hacking back at an attacker is self-defence."** Breaking into an attacker's computer is still breaking into a computer, and it could be a wrong address or someone else's machine they borrowed. Reporting it is the owner's call, and investigating is a job for the police.
- **"Nobody would ever notice."** Security teams keep logs of who connects to what. Your team's defender, Idris, reads them for a living.
