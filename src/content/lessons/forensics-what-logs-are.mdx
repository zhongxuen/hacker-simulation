## The one-sentence version

A <Term id="log-file">log file</Term> is a diary a computer keeps: a program writes one line every time something happens, with the date and time in front.

Nobody sat down to write it. Each line was added by whichever program was doing the work at that moment. So a computer doesn't keep one diary, it keeps a shelf of them, one for each writer. Run `logview` with nothing after it and you see the shelf:

Two files, two points of view, and neither one holds the whole story. An investigation nearly always means reading several logs and laying them side by side.

## Why it matters in security

Nobody watches a computer at 2 in the morning. The logs do, and they keep writing while an intruder works, because the programs writing them have no idea anything is wrong.

That makes logs the raw material of <Term id="digital-forensics">digital forensics</Term>: working out what happened after the fact. A line nobody thought twice about on Tuesday becomes the first solid fact on Friday. It also makes them <Term id="digital-evidence">digital evidence</Term>, which is a higher bar than "useful": a line can only be relied on if you can show it hasn't been altered since it was written.

## How it actually works

A log line is written by a program, to a file, at the moment something happens. Three parts of that are worth holding on to.

**Who writes.** On a Linux computer, most writers keep their diaries in the `/var/log` folder. The usual cast:

- The operating system: the computer starting up, disks filling, hardware complaining.
- Services that answer other computers: sign-ins, web requests, database queries.
- Applications: whatever the program's authors decided was worth recording.
- The network gear around the computer: a firewall writing down which messages it let through, and which it blocked.

Each one sees a slice. The sign-in service can tell you that an account signed in at 02:14; only the web server can tell you what that account then asked for.

**When.** Every line starts with a <Term id="timestamp">timestamp</Term>, and that's what lets you lay two diaries side by side. It's also the part most likely to mislead you, which is what [Putting events in order](/learn/forensics-timelines) is about.

**What, and what not.** A program writes what its authors and its settings told it to write. Turn logging down to save disk space and the lines stop, with no warning to anyone. So a missing line means "nothing recorded this", never "this didn't happen".

Some of what you find becomes an <Term id="indicator-of-compromise">indicator of compromise</Term>: a concrete clue other teams can check their own computers for, like an address that keeps coming back, or the name of an account nobody remembers creating.

Read `syslog` and you get a scheduled job finishing and a disk filling up: a quiet morning. Read `auth.log` and you get three refused passwords for `admin` at 02:14. Same computer, same night, two different stories, and you need both to know which one to worry about.

Every option `logview` takes is on its [manual page](/learn/commands#logview), [Reading logs](/learn/linux-logs) goes through a single line word by word, and [Don't change what you're looking at](/learn/forensics-evidence-care) covers how to handle a log you may need to rely on later.

## Common misconceptions

- **"There's a log of everything."** There's a log of everything somebody set up a log for. Plenty of programs write nothing at all, and plenty of settings quietly reduce what gets written.
- **"The events that matter are the ones marked WARN or ERROR."** Those words are the writing program's opinion, formed long before your investigation. The sign-in that cost a bakery a weekend was marked INFO, because from the sign-in service's point of view it worked perfectly.
- **"One log tells the story."** One log tells one program's side of it. The story lives in the gaps between diaries, which is why the next thing you learn is how to line them up.
- **"Deleting the log deletes the evidence."** A missing file, or a gap in one, is a finding all by itself. Teams also copy logs to a separate computer as they're written, so that deleting the original changes nothing.
- **"Logs are only read after an attack."** Most log reading is unglamorous: why is this slow, where did that print job go. The habit is what makes the 2 a.m. lines recognisable when they turn up.
