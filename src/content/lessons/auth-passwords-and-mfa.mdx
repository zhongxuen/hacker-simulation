## The one-sentence version

A strong <Term id="password">password</Term> is a long one you haven't used anywhere else — and a second step at sign-in protects you even on the day that password is stolen.

Think of a key and a doorman. A longer key is harder to copy. But a doorman who knows your face stops the person who copied it anyway, and that's a different kind of protection altogether.

## Why it matters in security

Stolen passwords are how most break-ins start. Not clever tricks — someone signs in with a password that worked.

They get them in three ways, and each has a different defence:

- **Guessing at the door.** A program tries password after password against a sign-in page. A <Term id="dictionary-attack">dictionary attack</Term> tries the popular ones first; a <Term id="brute-force-attack">brute-force attack</Term> grinds through combinations. Both leave a trail of failed sign-ins in the logs, which is one of the easiest attacks to spot.
- **Guessing offline.** Someone steals a password file and guesses against it on their own machines, quietly, with no failed sign-ins to notice. Strong storage is what limits the damage here.
- **Asking you for it.** <Term id="phishing">Phishing</Term>: a convincing email, a sign-in page that looks right, and you hand it over yourself. No amount of password length helps, because you typed it in willingly.

Then there's the multiplier: **reuse**. One password across ten sites means the weakest of those ten decides the fate of your email, and criminals take stolen lists and try them everywhere else. This is why a break-in at a shop you barely remember can cost someone their bank account.

## How it actually works

### What makes a password strong

Guessing programs don't work alphabetically. They start with the passwords people actually pick — the leaked lists, the seasons, the pet names, the keyboard runs — then apply the substitutions humans think are clever. What defeats them is being outside the patterns they expect.

Which comes down to three things:

1. **Length, above all.** Every extra character multiplies the work. A passphrase of four or five unrelated words is long _and_ memorable, which no amount of punctuation manages.
2. **Not reused.** A password used in one place can only ever be stolen from one place.
3. **Not derived from you.** Your dog, your street, your birth year and your team are all public, and guessing programs are fed exactly that kind of list.

Nobody can remember thirty passphrases, so the honest advice is a password manager: it invents a long random password per site, remembers them, and you learn one strong passphrase to unlock it. That's a much smaller thing to ask of a person.

### What a second step adds

<Term id="two-factor-authentication">Two-factor authentication</Term> asks for proof of two
different *kinds*:

- Something you **know** — a password.
- Something you **have** — a code from your phone, an app, or a small key you plug in.
- Something you **are** — a fingerprint or a face.

Two passwords aren't two factors; they're one factor twice, and both fall to the same stolen file. The strength comes from the _kinds_ being different, so one theft can't produce both.

Here's the uncomfortable arithmetic. A twenty-character passphrase is essentially unguessable — and if you type it into a convincing fake sign-in page, it's gone in a second, and its length never came into it. A second step is the only one of these two that still helps on that day.

## In practice

Client advice, in the order that helps most: turn on a second step for email and anything holding money, stop sharing one password between people, give everyone a password manager, and set a lockout after repeated failures. Notice that "make everyone's password longer" isn't first — it's real, and the other four do more.

<Term id="authentication">Authentication</Term> is the question "who are you?", and every one of
these makes the answer harder to fake. In [Reading the machine](/missions/linux-01) you'll meet the
version of this problem that small organisations actually have: one password, written down, shared
by everyone, and no way to tell who used it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Changing your password every 90 days makes you safer."** It was standard advice for years, and studies of real workplaces found it backfires: people shuffle to `Bakery1`, `Bakery2`, `Bakery3`. Current guidance is a long unique password kept until there's a reason to change it — and a reason means a break-in, not a date.
- **"Symbols and numbers are what make a password strong."** Length is what makes it strong. `Xk7$!q` is short; `correct-oven-harbour-sandwich` is not, and it's the one you'll remember on a Monday morning.
- **"Nobody would bother with my account."** Almost nothing here is aimed at you personally. Programs try millions of stolen passwords against every site they can reach, and your account is on the list because it exists.
- **"Two-factor is a nuisance for no benefit."** It's the single biggest drop in account break-ins any organisation can buy, usually in an afternoon. A few seconds at sign-in, weighed against someone reading everything in your email.
- **"Codes by text message are pointless if they can be intercepted."** Text-message codes are the weakest kind of second step, and still far better than none. An app or a plug-in key is stronger; the wrong move is turning the whole thing off because one version isn't perfect.
- **"A password manager puts all my eggs in one basket."** It does concentrate the risk, and that's the honest objection. It also removes reuse, which is the thing actually costing people their accounts — one well-guarded basket beats thirty scattered ones, especially with a second step on the basket.
