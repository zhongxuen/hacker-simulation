## The one-sentence version

A website has a terrible memory: every request arrives as if from a stranger. So when you sign in, it gives your browser a small note to show on every visit after that. The note is a <Term id="cookie">cookie</Term>, and the time you stay signed in is a <Term id="session">session</Term>.

It's like a cloakroom ticket. You hand over your coat once, get a numbered ticket, and show the ticket to collect it. Here's a practice sign-in page handing out its ticket:

## Why it matters in security

Once you've signed in, the session cookie is what proves it's you. The website doesn't ask for your password again: it checks the ticket. So anyone who copies your ticket can walk up to the cloakroom as you, without ever knowing your password.

That's why attackers try to steal session cookies, and why defenders go to a lot of trouble to protect them.

## How it actually works

1. You sign in with your password. Proving who you are like this is called <Term id="authentication">authentication</Term>.
2. The website creates a session on its side, gives it a long random number, and sends that number back in a `Set-Cookie` header.
3. Your browser stores the cookie and sends it with every request to that website, in a `Cookie` header.
4. The website looks the number up and knows it's you, until you sign out or the session runs out.

<Term id="http">HTTP</Term> itself forgets everything between requests, so this ticket is how almost
every website keeps you signed in.

The extra words after the cookie are rules for the browser:

| Rule       | What it tells the browser                                                                |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `HttpOnly` | Don't let scripts on the page read this cookie. That blocks a common way of stealing it. |
| `Secure`   | Only send it over HTTPS, the scrambled version of HTTP, never as readable text.          |
| `SameSite` | Don't send it when another website makes the request.                                    |
| `Expires`  | Throw it away after this date. Without it, the cookie goes when the browser closes.      |

Where the cloakroom analogy breaks down: a good website can notice odd use of a ticket, like one number suddenly appearing from another country, and cancel it.

## In practice

When you probe a sign-in page with `webprobe`, check the `Set-Cookie` line: are `HttpOnly` and `Secure` there? A session cookie without them is a finding worth a line in a report. On the defending side, <Term id="two-factor-authentication">two-factor authentication</Term> makes stolen passwords far less useful, and short sessions make stolen tickets expire quickly.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Cookies are viruses."** A cookie is a small piece of text a website asks your browser to keep. It can't run or spread. The worry with cookies is privacy and stolen sessions, not infection.
- **"Deleting cookies makes me invisible."** It signs you out and forgets preferences, but websites can still see your address and other details of each request.
- **"The cookie holds my password."** A well-built site never puts the password in a cookie. It stores a random ticket number, which is useless once the session ends.
