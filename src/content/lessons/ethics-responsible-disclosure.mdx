## The one-sentence version

<Term id="responsible-disclosure">Responsible disclosure</Term> means telling a system's owner
privately about a weakness you found, and giving them time to fix it before anyone else hears about
it.

A weak spot in a computer system is called a <Term id="vulnerability">vulnerability</Term>. What you do after finding one matters as much as finding it. Try this one:

## Why it matters in security

A weakness that's public but not fixed is a map for attackers. Announcing it too early can hurt the owner, and it can hurt their customers, whose data sits behind that weak spot.

Keeping it secret forever isn't right either. People deserve to have problems fixed. Responsible disclosure is the middle path: the owner gets a fair chance to fix it, and then, if everyone agrees, the lessons can be shared.

## How it actually works

Inside a paid test, like the ones your team does, the path is agreed in advance. Everything you find goes into a report for the client, and it stays between you and them.

Outside a test, when someone stumbles on a weakness in a website they use, the usual steps look like this:

1. **Stop.** Don't dig further or try to prove it by breaking in.
2. **Find the right contact.** Many organisations publish how they want to hear about problems, sometimes in a file called `security.txt` on their website. Some run programmes that reward reports, often called bug bounties.
3. **Report privately**, with what you saw and how to repeat it safely.
4. **Give them time.** A common arrangement is around 90 days, agreed with the owner, to build and release a fix, called a <Term id="patch">patch</Term>.
5. **Share later, if agreed.** After the fix, researchers sometimes publish what they learned so others can avoid the same mistake.

Careful: finding something by accident doesn't give you permission to test it further. Stop at what you saw.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Posting a bug publicly is the only way to make them fix it."** Most owners fix problems once they hear about them privately. Going public first puts their customers at risk while they scramble.
- **"If I found it by accident, I'm allowed to keep testing it."** Stumbling on something doesn't grant permission. Report what you saw and stop there.
- **"A reward programme means anything goes."** Bug bounty programmes have their own rules about what may be tested and how. Outside those rules, it's testing without permission.
- **"Reporting a problem gets you in trouble."** Many organisations welcome reports and say so publicly. Following their published process, and not digging further, is what keeps a report on the right side of the line.
