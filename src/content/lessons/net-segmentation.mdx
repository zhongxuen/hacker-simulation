## The one-sentence version

Instead of one big network where every device can reach every other, you split it into separate zones with rules between them. That's <Term id="network-segmentation">network segmentation</Term>.

Think of a restaurant. Customers sit in the dining room, staff work in the kitchen, and the safe is in a locked office. Everyone is in the same building, but a customer can't wander into the kitchen. Pick each zone of a small bakery's network to see who belongs where:

## Why it matters in security

On a flat network, where everything can reach everything, one weak device is a way in to all of them. A guest's infected laptop on the wifi can knock on the doors of the order server. Segmentation means that when something goes wrong in one zone, and eventually something will, the damage stays there.

It also makes trouble stand out. If the rules say customer wifi never talks to the servers, then any attempt to do it is worth an alert.

## How it actually works

Zones are usually separate <Term id="subnet">subnets</Term>: different streets of addresses. Messages between streets pass through a <Term id="router">router</Term>, the device that forwards messages from one network to another, like a sorting office. That's the natural place to check them.

The checking is done by a <Term id="firewall">firewall</Term>: a list of rules saying who may talk to whom, and on which ports. Rules are read top to bottom, and the last one usually says "everything else is blocked".

You can only write good rules if you know what's on the network, which is why segmentation starts with an <Term id="asset-inventory">asset inventory</Term>: a list of every device, and which zone it belongs in.

Where the restaurant analogy breaks down: anyone can see a kitchen door, but network zones are invisible. A single wrong rule can quietly join two zones back together, and nobody notices until someone scans.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A firewall makes you safe."** A firewall is only as good as its rules. A rule that allows everything, or one left over from years ago, lets trouble straight through.
- **"Segmentation is only for big companies."** A bakery with customer wifi needs it as much as a bank. Many home and shop routers can make a separate guest network with one setting.
- **"Guests on the wifi can't see anything anyway."** On a flat network they can knock on every door, the same as a staff laptop. A scan from the wifi would list the till and the servers.
- **"Once the network is split, the job's done."** New devices arrive, rules pile up, and zones drift together. Teams check the rules and rescan regularly.
