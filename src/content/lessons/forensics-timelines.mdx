## The one-sentence version

A <Term id="timeline">timeline</Term> is every event you can prove, from every log you have, laid out in the order it actually happened.

The order events happened in is not the order they sit in a file. Here are all six lines of the sign-in log from a practice computer, exactly as they're stored:

Files end up out of order all the time: logs get merged, copied back after a restore, or written by programs that batch things up. Sort by the time in the line, never by where the line sits.

## Why it matters in security

An investigation is a sentence with the word "then" in it. _Someone tried three passwords, then one worked, then a file left the building._ Without the order, you have three facts and no story, and you can't tell a client what happened or what to change.

Order is also how you spot the difference between cause and effect. If the outgoing traffic started before the sign-in, the sign-in isn't what caused it, and you're still looking for the way in.

## How it actually works

Building a timeline is four steps, and the messy one is in the middle.

**1. Gather.** Every source that might have seen the event: the machine itself, the firewall in front of it, the service it talks to, the door-entry system if the story involves a room.

**2. Normalise.** Every line carries a <Term id="timestamp">timestamp</Term>, and the first job is to put every one of them on the same clock before comparing anything. Most servers record in <Term id="coordinated-universal-time">coordinated universal time</Term>, one world clock, which is what the `Z` on the end of `2026-03-02T02:14:07Z` means. Anything recorded in local time gets converted. Anything without a time zone at all gets a note saying so, because a guess written down as a fact is how investigations go wrong.

**3. Sort.** One list, earliest first. Each <Term id="log-entry">log entry</Term> keeps a note of which file it came from, so you can go back and read it in context.

**4. Read it as a story,** and keep going back past the event that started the investigation. The alert is rarely the beginning. The beginning is usually a dull line, days earlier, that nobody had a reason to look at.

### The two things that break timelines

**Clocks that disagree.** A machine running four minutes fast records everything four minutes early, so an effect can appear to happen before its cause. That slow slide is <Term id="clock-drift">clock drift</Term>, and it's why an investigator checks what each machine thought the time was before trusting the order of anything. Sometimes the drift is deliberate: changing a clock is a way to make records confusing.

**Logs that ran out.** <Term id="log-rotation">Log rotation</Term> starts a fresh file every so often and deletes the oldest, so records never fill the disk. If a company keeps seven days and the way in was opened three weeks ago, that part of the timeline is gone. Finding out how far back the records go is worth doing early, before you spend a day looking for something that no longer exists.

## See it

The practice log you read above is out of order. Put it right.

<MiniTerminal
  scenario="range-logs"
  commands={[
    "logview /var/log/auth.log",
    "sort /var/log/auth.log",
    "logview /var/log/syslog",
    "date",
  ]}
  task="Run `sort /var/log/auth.log` to put the lines in time order."
  expect="sort"
  success="Now the night reads properly: three refused passwords at 02:14 come first, and the morning follows. Sorting worked because every line starts with its time, written longest unit first."
/>

That ordering is a small gift from the way the times are written. `2026-03-02T02:14:07Z` puts the year first, then the month, then the day: sorting the text alphabetically sorts it by time as well. A log written `2/3/26 2:14am` sorts into nonsense, which is one reason the long form is the one to use.

`date` tells you what this machine thinks the time is. On a real investigation that's step one, on every machine you touch.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The order in the file is the order it happened."** Files get merged, restored and written out of sequence. Sort on the time in the line.
- **"A timestamp is a fact."** It's what one machine's clock believed at the time, which is a different thing. Two records four minutes apart may be the same event seen by a machine that's running fast.
- **"If two logs disagree, one of them is lying."** Time zones and drift explain nearly every disagreement you'll meet. Convert to one clock, check the clocks, and see what's left.
- **"The timeline stops at the alert."** The alert is where you came in. The first interesting event is usually earlier and quieter, and finding it is the point of the exercise.
- **"Same second, same moment, so the order doesn't matter."** Two events a fraction apart can land in the same second. When the order matters and the seconds can't settle it, say so in the report instead of picking one.
